HAD.

Human–AI Design: designing a home in conversation with an AI that can act on the plan.

Conversational design · Typed intent · Deterministic solvers · 2026

Summary

Human–AI Design (HAD) is a prototype for designing a home in conversation with an AI. You sketch; the assistant sees your plan, asks what you are designing, talks it through with you, and can act on it: furnishing a room, arranging a kit, moving what is there, and explaining each decision in words you can check and undo. I developed it, as its sole designer and developer, to explore how people and AI can design together when models interpret and computational tools decide.

This page follows one request through that collaboration: how the plan becomes something a model can understand, how intent becomes a form its tools can act on, and how the person stays the author. Placing a bed is the worked example; the subject is the hand-off.

01 — The question✦

Can you just put the bed in the bedroom?

Picture people designing a home in conversation: they describe, point, reconsider. “Bed against the long wall, nightstands either side, desk facing the window” is a complete instruction for a person who can see the room. A drafting tool still needs positions and angles before those words can become part of the plan.

Language models can now hold that conversation fluently, which sharpens the question: who decides where the bed actually goes? I developed HAD to find out what a design conversation becomes when the AI on the other side can act: let the model interpret, and give it tools that can place. Setting up that hand-off began with understanding what went wrong first.

02 — The placement problem✦

Fluent in words, blind to the wall

The first version’s assistant could discuss the plan, but its only tool named an item; it had to tell the person where to click. It never emitted coordinates, yet described positions nobody could draw, sent a desk and chair to opposite corners, and knew nothing of wall lengths or door swings. The model was doing its job; the tool surface was the bug.

The realtime model is weak at spatial math, so I stopped asking it to do any. The new contract passes intent in a small vocabulary to a deterministic solver: a tool that computes positions and angles from the plan itself. When people said that confirming each proposal by click felt like the assistant was suggesting rather than placing, I let placements commit on their own, with undo. First, the solver needed a room it could reason about.

The model was doing its job. The tool was the bug.

— The decision behind HAD
03 — Building the hand-off✦

Walls become rooms

To understand the plan it discusses, the assistant needs rooms. People draw walls as strokes at any angle, including partitions that simply end against another wall. Nobody draws a room. HAD joins wall ends that nearly meet, splits a wall where another lands on it, and walks the resulting network to find every closed face: a room with its area, centre, and surrounding walls.

Take a bedroom 4.0 by 3.6 m, with a door on its south wall and a window on its east wall. The room is recomputed whenever the plan changes, never stored. Each opening remembers its wall and its position along it, so it moves or disappears with that wall.

Together, these relationships form the design model, the structured description of the plan that the rest of HAD works from. Its knowledge of rooms comes from the walls people draw and the openings attached to them. The next question was how to describe that information to a language model.

The scene, told three ways

I prepared scene context, a task-specific description of the current plan, so the assistant can understand what it is discussing. For conversation, HAD tells the plan as an architect might: which room, how large, which openings, what is inside. That description connects the conversation to the drawing.

Layout planning needs a survey in 10 cm cells, including the free space on each side of existing objects. A precise move needs a smaller, exact brief about one object, its neighbours, and the room’s safe interior rectangle. Each model also receives a canvas screenshot.

The door’s swing arcs, the window’s view cones, and the rules about what may stand against a wall stay with the solver, because it is the part that can be held to them. Tell each task what it needs, and no more. What the model may say back is just as deliberate.

Intent as a form

A typed instruction is a form with a limited vocabulary for expressing intent. It can ask for a wall, choosing the longest, shortest, or a specific one; a corner; the centre; a named side of another piece; somewhere near something; or a position between two things. There is no field for coordinates.

Placement can face into the room, toward the door, toward the window, toward another object, or match its anchor. References identify a piece by name, by the current selection, or as “the piece before this one.” These choices let the model express relationships that the solver can resolve.

“Put a queen bed in the bedroom” becomes an item, a room, the longest unobstructed wall, and a direction into the room. “And a nightstand on the left” refers back to that bed and names its left side. The model has interpreted; it has not measured. Measuring is the solver’s job.

The solver decides exactly where

The solver ranks candidate walls by length, longest first. Ties are broken by preferences such as being away from the door, then by a stable order, so the same request in the same room gives the same answer. It poses this bed against the wall, facing into the room.

It checks that the bed stays inside the room without overlapping walls or furniture. For wall-anchored items, it tests the door’s swing arc and protects the window’s view from tall pieces. Here, south and north tie in length, but the door’s swing takes the southern spot; the bed goes north.

If a wall fails, the solver tries the next. For a kit, pieces are solved in dependency order against a scene already holding the earlier ones, and anything that does not fit is reported by name. Each placement changes what is available to the next.

A single item that fits nowhere on its requested anchor instead goes to the nearest open spot, and the assistant says so. That fallback matters: the result can differ from the requested position. From the person’s side, the machinery gives way to something simpler: a placement they can see and undo.

04 — Using it✦

Say it, see it, undo it

Before placing anything, the assistant asks what you are designing and which room to focus on. It keeps that goal in view as you talk about the room, whether by voice or text. The request grows out of that conversation.

When you ask for the bed, it appears with a small AI badge that fades after a few seconds. The assistant explains its position in ordinary words: against the longest clear wall, facing into the room. Select it later and the inspector shows which tier and tool placed it, and when.

“Set up the office” arrives as one batch, and one undo removes the whole kit. The bed and the nightstand, requested separately, take two undos. I wanted the unit of reversal to follow the action, so a room kit remains something a person can readily take back.

I kept structure on the slower path: walls, doors, windows, and labels are placed by your click. The assistant can hand you a door and name the wall; you decide where along it. The assistant furnishes; you draw. Both work on the same design model, which makes the next part possible.

05 — One model, three surfaces✦

The same plan, walked

One click turns the design model into a walkthrough: each wall becomes boxes split around its openings, and each detected room becomes a floor. Nothing is modelled twice, so the bed placed on the plan is the bed you walk around. The voice session started in 2D stays open in 3D, so you can keep talking while you walk the plan.

On a headset, the same scene is a walkthrough, not an editing surface: you can still undo from the controller and keep talking to the assistant, but there are no tools for drawing or moving things. I kept editing on the desktop on purpose, where the canvas supports precision. Three surfaces share one model, and one question follows them: what did the system actually check?

06 — Keeping it honest✦

Not every edit takes the same road

The assistant’s actions take different roads: solver-backed placement and relational moves check containment and overlap, with swing and view checks for wall anchors. “Turn the desk to face into the room” expresses a move the solver can resolve. If the proposed move fails, the model gets one more attempt with the exact violation; success is not guaranteed.

Small nudges apply the model’s own change with lighter checks: a rotation is refused if it would hit a wall, a resize is clamped, and a nudge simply moves. Walls and openings follow your click. For catalog items, the model draws the icon and 3D shape but never chooses their position in a room.

The session recorder keeps every prompt, every instruction, and every solver verdict in order. A recording of a desk rotated into a wall without a retry is why rotations are now checked first. I used those traces to hold claims to implemented behaviour, the same standard this page needs to meet.

Checked against what is implemented, not promised against everything.

— The rule behind the claims
07 — What it adds up to✦

Structured design information, connected to AI operations

The person draws, judges, and decides; the model listens and interprets; structured tools resolve and check. That division is what Human–AI Design names. I developed HAD around it so that discussing a design can lead to an action on the plan, while the person remains its author.

For the person, the design conversation continues through actions they can question, inspect, and reverse. For me, it is a pattern I keep reaching for: models interpret, structured tools decide, wherever geometry, rules, or records deserve precision. “Can you just put the bed in the bedroom?” now has an answer in the plan, with the placement explained and the decision reversible.

Project at a glance

What problem it targets

Designing in conversation with an AI requires an assistant that understands the plan, acts without guessing geometry, and explains actions people can reverse.

  • Understanding the drawing being discussed
  • Turning design intent into actions through computational tools
  • Keeping decisions explained, inspectable, and reversible

What I developed

  • A voice and text design assistant that sees the plan, asks about the goal, and acts through tools
  • Design model with derived rooms and parametric doors and windows
  • Task-specific scene context for conversation, planning, and precise moves
  • Typed placement vocabulary and deterministic solvers with implemented checks
  • Visible, undoable AI commits with provenance across 2D, 3D, and headset surfaces

Core tech

  • Design model: TypeScript document validated with Zod; rooms derived by wall-network face tracing; parametric doors and windows
  • Spatial computation: Deterministic placement and transform solvers: wall ranking, dependency-ordered kits, door-swing and window-view clearance, overlap and containment checks; seeded randomized tests for overlap, containment and repeat determinism
  • AI: gpt-realtime-2 over WebRTC for conversation and tool calls; GPT-5-class and Claude models for layout planning, gpt-5.2 by default; claude-haiku-4-5 for relational moves; typed outputs checked at every boundary
  • Surfaces: React and SVG editor; three.js walkthrough; WebXR walkthrough for Quest browsers
  • Platform: Cloudflare Workers; Supabase with row-level security; Vitest, 600+ tests
